# Psychology lens

People move through every screen in four steps: they **filter** what they see, **make meaning** of it, **act** under time pressure, and **remember** some of it afterward. Design fails when it ignores one of those steps. Use this lens to explain *why* a design works or fails, not as a checklist to sprinkle onto screens.

The four-step framing and much of the vocabulary follow the Growth.Design principles library (growth.design/psychology), which in turn borrows its four categories from Buster Benson's Cognitive Bias Codex. The wording below is a working summary for designers, not a copy of either source. Go to the original for case studies and examples.

## Ethics rule (read first)

Use psychology to **reduce friction, confusion and effort**. Do not use it to pressure people. Scarcity, loss aversion, sunk cost, variable reward and social proof can all be turned against users. If a tactic works *because* the person doesn't notice it, or because it makes them feel anxious, it fails this rule. When a principle could be used both ways, name the honest use and skip the manipulative one. Reactance is the reminder: people push back when they feel forced, and they remember it.

## 1. Filter: what gets noticed

People skim, ignore repeated things, and read the first thing they see as the reference point.

| Principle | What it means when designing | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive load | Every element costs mental effort; effort is a budget | What can be removed without losing the task? |
| Hick's law | More choices means slower, worse decisions | Are there more than ~5 peer options at the moment of decision? |
| Progressive disclosure | Show the core now, reveal the rest when asked for | Is anything advanced visible to a first-time user? |
| Visual hierarchy / contrast | Attention goes to the heaviest thing first | If I squint, is the most important thing the most prominent? |
| Von Restorff effect | The one different thing is the one noticed | Is exactly one thing distinct, and is it the right one? |
| Law of proximity / similarity | Near or alike means related | Do grouping and spacing say what belongs together? |
| Banner blindness | Repeated or ad-like elements get tuned out | Does the critical message look like decoration? |
| Anchoring / framing | The first number or phrasing colours everything after | What is the first thing they see, and is it fair? |
| Fitts's law | Big and near is easy to hit | Is the primary action large, and reachable by a thumb? |
| Signifiers | Elements should say what they do | Can someone tell what is tappable without guessing? |
| Tesler's law | Complexity can't be removed, only moved | Did we simplify by pushing work onto the user? |

## 2. Make meaning: what people assume

People fill gaps with assumptions drawn from other products and the physical world.

| Principle | What it means when designing | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Mental model | People arrive with a picture of how it works | Where does our structure differ from what they expect? |
| Familiarity bias | Known patterns feel easier | Are we being different for a reason the user benefits from? |
| Curse of knowledge | We forget what it's like not to know | Would a stranger understand this label? |
| Feedforward | Tell people what will happen before they act | Does the button say what happens next? |
| Feedback loop | Tell people what happened after they act | Does every action produce a visible, specific result? |
| Aha moment | The instant someone sees the value | How many steps until the first real value? |
| Goal gradient | Motivation rises near the goal | Do they see how close they are? |
| Occam's razor | The simpler explanation or design usually wins | Is there a simpler way to reach the same outcome? |
| Aesthetic-usability effect | Attractive looks easier, and hides problems in testing | Are we mistaking polish for clarity? |
| Social proof, scarcity | Strong levers, easy to abuse | Is the claim true and useful, or just pushy? |

## 3. Act: what happens under time pressure

People are busy, distracted and lazy in the good sense: they take the path of least effort and stay with defaults.

| Principle | What it means when designing | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Default bias | Most people keep the default | Is the default good for the user, not just for us? |
| Decision fatigue | Every decision spends energy | Can we decide for them, or defer the decision? |
| Discoverability | A feature nobody finds doesn't exist | How does someone learn this is here? |
| Spark effect / Fogg-style small steps | Tiny first steps get taken | What is the smallest first action? |
| Commitment and consistency | Small yeses lead to bigger ones | Is the first ask small and honest? |
| Reactance | Force creates resistance | Does anything feel like a trap? |
| Labor illusion | Visible work builds trust in slow processes | If it takes time, do we show what's happening? |
| Second-order effect | Consequences of the consequences | What does this cause a week from now? |
| Loss aversion, sunk cost | Powerful and easy to exploit | Are we protecting users from loss, or threatening them with it? |

## 4. Remember: what sticks

People remember peaks, endings and unfinished business, and forget the middle.

| Principle | What it means when designing | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Peak-end rule | The best or worst moment and the ending define the memory | What is the last screen they see, and how do they feel? |
| Zeigarnik effect | Unfinished tasks nag | Is progress saved and resumable? |
| Chunking | Grouped information is easier to hold | Are long lists broken into meaningful groups? |
| Recognition over recall | Recognizing beats remembering | Do we make people remember something the screen could show? |
| Endowment effect | Owned things feel more valuable | Does the person see their own data and choices reflected? |
| Delighters | Small unexpected pleasures are remembered | Is there one considered moment, not a dozen? |
| Provide exit points | Let people leave at a natural moment | Is there a good place to stop? |

## How to use it

- Name the principle **and** the specific thing on the screen it explains. "Hick's law" alone is decoration; "eight equal filters at the top of the wardrobe list slow the first decision" is a finding.
- One or two principles per decision is usually enough. Citing ten is a sign none were used.
- A principle is a hypothesis. Where it matters, say how you would test it.
