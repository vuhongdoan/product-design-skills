# The four budgets, and character

These budgets are the quality bar. They exist because four failures show up again and again, in human and AI work alike: the design ignores real life, it looks tidy but is hard to follow, too many things compete for attention, and motion is overused. Each budget turns one failure into a limit you can check.

**Restraint is about noise, not character.** The budgets cut clutter, confusion and decoration that serves no one. They never ask for a plain, cautious or anonymous design. A page can pass every budget and still fail because it's forgettable, text-heavy or could belong to any brand. That failure is covered in section 5, and it counts as much as the other four.

A budget is a default, not a law. You may exceed one when the brief needs it, but say so, and say why the person using the product benefits.

## 1. Context budget: design for real life

Before designing, know the situation the product is used in (see `context-brief.md`). A design that ignores it is wrong however good it looks.

Check every screen and flow against:
- **Where and when**: on a train, in bed, at a desk, in a shop, in bright sun?
- **Hands and attention**: one hand, interrupted every ten seconds, half-watching?
- **State of mind**: rushed, anxious, bored, curious, in the middle of a task for someone else?
- **Connection and device**: slow or no network, low battery, small or old phone, screen reader, large text?
- **Interruption**: what happens if they leave for an hour and come back? Is their work still there?
- **What else is competing**: the room, the kids, the other app, the notification?

The output is a written **context statement** at the top of the work, and a real-life stress test at the end: "still works one-handed, offline and interrupted?"

## 2. Comprehension budget: clear, not just tidy

A screen is understandable when a first-time user can answer three questions without help: *What is this? What can I do here? What happens if I do it?*

- The **five-second test**: show the screen for five seconds, then ask what it is for and what to do next. If they can't say, the design fails regardless of polish.
- Labels use the user's words, not the system's. Buttons say what will happen ("Save outfit", not "Submit").
- Every element that looks interactive is, and every interactive element looks like it.
- The next step is obvious. The design never leaves the person wondering whether something worked.
- Neatness is not clarity. Alignment, consistent spacing and a clean palette can hide a confusing structure. When judging, ask "could I use this?" before "does this look right?".
- **Show before you tell.** People understand a picture, a demo or a thing they can try faster than a paragraph. When a section is mostly text, ask what could show it instead.

## 3. Attention budget: one thing at a time

Attention is limited, so spend it deliberately.

- **One primary action per screen.** If two things feel equally important, one of them isn't, or the screen should be two screens.
- **One focal point at a time**: at any scroll position or moment, one thing clearly leads. That leading thing can be big, colourful and bold. The budget limits *how many things shout*, not how loud the one thing is.
- Aim for **no more than five to seven peer items** in a group at the point of decision; group or hide the rest (progressive disclosure).
- Anything that is not needed for the current task moves off the screen: to a later step, a detail view, or out.
- Badges, banners, promos and notifications are attention spending too. Count them.

Test: point at the screen and ask "where does the eye go first, second, third?" If the answer is "everywhere", cut.

## 4. Motion budget: every motion has a job

Motion is useful when it tells or gives the person something. It is harmful when it only decorates or repeats.

Jobs motion can do:
- **Answer an action**: a press, a toggle, a sheet opening, a drag.
- **Show what changed**: an item moving to a new place, something saved or removed.
- **Show progress** on something slow.
- **Explain how the product works**: a short demo, a before-and-after, a step that assembles itself.
- **Let people play**: direct manipulation, sliders, a configurator, a thing that responds to the pointer.
- **Carry the brand**: one signature moment per page or flow that people remember (peak-end rule).

Not allowed:
- Entrance animations on every section, staggered fade-ups as a default, parallax and scroll-jacking for their own sake, looping pulses, springs and hover effects on everything, motion that delays reading.

Rules:
- Interface feedback is quick (roughly 150–300 ms). Longer motion needs a job from the list above.
- **Never the only carrier of information**. It must make sense without it.
- Always respect the user's reduced-motion setting, and provide a still equivalent.

## 5. Character: it should be unmistakably this product

A design passes the budgets and still fails if it's generic. Check:

- **The cover-the-logo test**: hide the name. Would you know whose product this is? Could a competitor use the same page by swapping the logo? If yes, it has no character.
- **A concept**: one idea drawn from the subject (its world, materials, place, rituals, vocabulary) that shapes the type, colour, imagery and interaction. Not a style picked from a trend list.
- **A signature element**: at least one thing people would describe to a friend: an interactive demo, an illustration system, a striking type treatment, a product mockup that tells a story.
- **Visual richness in the right places**: imagery, illustration, product visuals and colour that do work (explain, show, create feeling). Text walls, all-grey palettes, default system type on a brand page and hairline-rule layouts read as unfinished or dated.
- **Brand consistency**: the same voice, palette, type and motifs everywhere, so it adds up to an identity.

Character and the budgets pull in the same direction when done well: a strong concept gives you one clear focal point per section, and an interactive demo explains faster than copy.
