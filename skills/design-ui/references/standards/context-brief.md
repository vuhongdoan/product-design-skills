# Context brief

Every piece of design work starts from a **context brief**. It keeps the work tied to real people in real situations, and it stops each skill from re-asking the same questions. The lead skill creates it; every other skill reads it, and adds to it when it learns something new.

Look for `DESIGN-CONTEXT.md` in the working folder first. If it exists, read it and confirm anything that looks stale. If it doesn't, ask the questions below in **one message**, offer proposed answers for anything you can infer, and write the file. If the person won't or can't answer, draft it with clearly marked assumptions and carry on; a marked assumption is better than a stalled task.

## Rules for the brief

- **Both sides.** A brief with only the user side leads to designs nobody can sustain; one with only the business side leads to designs people resent. Fill in both, even roughly.
- **Specific beats broad.** "Commuters checking their outfit for tomorrow on the tram, one-handed" is useful. "Fashion-conscious users" is not.
- **Separate facts from assumptions.** Never write an assumption in the evidence section.
- **Keep it to one page.** If it's longer, nobody reads it.
- **Update, don't fork.** When new research or decisions land, edit the brief and note the date.
- **Product truth and brand inputs, not layouts.** Facts about the audience, situation and brand belong here, including existing brand assets. The creative direction and specific layouts belong in the design work, so the brief doesn't lock in early choices.
- **Ask about the brand before brand-facing work.** For marketing pages, first impressions, onboarding and anything new, the brand section matters as much as the product section. If it's empty, ask before designing.
